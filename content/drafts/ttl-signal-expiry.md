A spot recommendation built on 15-minute-old pricing data is not advice. It is historical trivia. AWS Spot prices change without warning, and a signal that suggested buying m5.large in us-east-1a five minutes ago may now reflect a market that no longer exists. RefineX expires every signal through a deterministic TTL system that ensures delivered recommendations reflect current conditions, not outdated snapshots.

## What Is Signal TTL?

Signal TTL (time-to-live) is the maximum age we allow a spot market signal to remain active before automatic expiration. Every signal generated by RefineX includes a TTL value measured in seconds and an explicit expires_at timestamp. When that deadline passes, the signal becomes inactive regardless of how attractive the underlying arbitrage opportunity appeared when first calculated.

Our expiration scheduler runs every 60 seconds, scanning the signal database for records where expires_at has passed and marking them inactive. This process removed 312 signals this week alone, maintaining data freshness across all delivered recommendations.

## How RefineX Calculates Signal Expiry

The TTL calculation happens at signal generation time, not delivery time. Each signal includes both a TTL duration in seconds and a specific expires_at timestamp derived from the signal creation time plus the calculated TTL period. The system stores these values directly in the signal database schema.

Our expire_signals.py worker runs as a scheduled job, querying for signals where the current timestamp exceeds the stored expires_at value. When expired signals are found, the system sets is_active to false, removing them from the active signal pool without deleting the historical record. This approach preserves the audit trail while preventing stale recommendations from reaching users.

The expiration logic operates independently of confidence scores or expected value calculations. A signal with 0.95 confidence and 60% savings potential expires at exactly the same moment as a 0.52 confidence signal if they share the same creation timestamp and TTL duration.

## Why Stale Pricing Data Breaks Spot Decisions

Spot pricing reflects real-time supply and demand across AWS availability zones. When demand spikes or capacity shifts, prices can double within minutes. A signal recommending m5.large in us-west-2b based on $0.045 pricing becomes meaningless if current rates have jumped to $0.089, pushing costs above on-demand levels.

We track this problem through suppression rates in our transparency log. During periods of rapid market repricing, TTL expiry rates spike as signals age out faster than normal market cycles. Last Tuesday, when AWS repriced several instance families across us-east regions, our expiry rate hit 67% as existing signals became obsolete before their standard lifecycle completed.

The alternative approach would be refreshing signal data in place, updating pricing while preserving the original recommendation. We reject this method because it obscures the decision timeline. A signal created at 14:22 with pricing from 14:37 misrepresents when the analysis actually occurred and what market conditions informed the original calculation.

## The 15-Minute Default TTL

RefineX sets a default 15-minute TTL for spot arbitrage signals, based on observed AWS pricing update frequencies across major regions. This duration provides sufficient time for signal delivery and user action while preventing recommendations from surviving major price movements.

The 15-minute window assumes a user workflow of signal receipt, internal approval or review, and infrastructure deployment or migration. Signals requiring longer consideration periods indicate a mismatch between market timing and organizational decision speed, not a problem with TTL configuration.

Some signals receive shorter TTL values based on market volatility indicators or recent pricing instability in the target region. When our analysis detects elevated price movement within the past hour, TTL drops to as low as 5 minutes to prevent delivery of recommendations likely to be outdated on arrival.

## Suppression Through TTL Expiry

When a signal expires through TTL, it appears in our public suppression log with the reason "ttl_expired" rather than confidence-based suppression. This distinction matters because TTL expiry indicates timing issues rather than signal quality problems. The underlying analysis may have been sound, but market timing made delivery inappropriate.

TTL-based suppression protects users from acting on recommendations that no longer reflect current conditions. Our transparency page shows both delivered and suppressed signals, including TTL expiry events, to provide complete visibility into the filtering process that occurs before signal delivery.

Current suppression rates show TTL expiry accounts for approximately 23% of all suppressed signals, with confidence-based suppression handling the remaining 77%. During volatile market periods, these ratios invert as rapid price changes make TTL the primary suppression mechanism.

Every expired signal preserves its original data in our database, including the pricing snapshot, confidence calculation, and expected value that informed the initial recommendation. This historical record enables analysis of market timing patterns and TTL effectiveness without compromising current signal freshness.
